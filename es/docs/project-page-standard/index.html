Project Page Standard

Purpose​

Projects on the IPI Research Platform are intended to make research directions understandable, navigable, and open to collaboration.

The purpose of this structure is not to impose rigidity, but to reduce unnecessary ambiguity and improve interoperability between projects.

Each project may organize itself differently internally, but a clear entry structure is strongly encouraged.

Recommended Project Structure

1. One-Line Summary​

A concise description of the project.

The summary should communicate:

  • the central idea,
  • the problem space,
  • or the research direction.

Example:

A framework exploring coherence and transformation through information geometry.

2. Central Intuition​

Describe the motivating intuition behind the project.

This section should answer:

  • What motivated the work?
  • What pattern or problem is being observed?
  • Why does the project exist?

This section may remain conceptual and does not require formalism.

3. Structural Problem​

Define the specific problem, limitation, contradiction, or research gap being addressed.

Examples:

  • conceptual fragmentation,
  • mathematical inconsistency,
  • explanatory limitations,
  • computational difficulty,
  • missing integration between fields,
  • insufficient models.

4. Key Concepts​

Define the important concepts used by the project.

Projects are encouraged to:

  • define terminology explicitly,
  • avoid unnecessary ambiguity,
  • and maintain internal consistency.

Concepts do not need to match terminology used by other projects.

5. Formal Structure​

This section contains technical content when applicable.

Possible contents:

  • mathematics,
  • models,
  • algorithms,
  • formal definitions,
  • simulations,
  • derivations,
  • logical structures.

Projects are encouraged to separate intuition from formal formalization whenever possible.

6. Current Status​

Describe the current maturity of the project.

Possible states may include:

  • exploratory,
  • conceptual,
  • partially formalized,
  • computational,
  • under validation,
  • incomplete,
  • speculative.

Clarity about the state of the work is more important than presenting artificial completeness.

7. Validation​

When applicable, projects should identify:

  • possible empirical tests,
  • computational validation,
  • logical consistency checks,
  • falsifiability conditions,
  • predictive consequences,
  • or simulation strategies.

Not all projects require immediate validation, but validation pathways should be discussed whenever possible.

8. Open Questions​

Projects are encouraged to expose unresolved problems explicitly.

Examples:

  • undefined structures,
  • mathematical gaps,
  • contradictory implications,
  • unknown limits,
  • unclear terminology,
  • unresolved assumptions.

Open questions help collaboration emerge naturally.

9. References​

Include:

  • papers,
  • books,
  • datasets,
  • frameworks,
  • related projects,
  • or external resources.

The objective is not only citation, but contextual integration.

10. Notes​

Projects may include exploratory notes outside the primary structure.

Notes may contain:

  • early intuitions,
  • partial derivations,
  • conceptual bridges,
  • unresolved observations,
  • or experimental ideas.

Notes are intended to preserve exploration without destabilizing the core structure of the project.
